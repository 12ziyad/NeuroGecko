# Leopard gecko surface anatomy, verified

Produced by a 42-agent research pass: one finder per surface feature, then
independent verifiers told to REFUTE each claim and to reject anything they
could not confirm from a source they had actually opened.

PARKED, NOT APPLIED. The skin work was called off before this landed, so
nothing here has been built. It is written down so the reading does not have
to be done twice.

10 claims survived verification; 26 were refuted or corrected.

## ear

**The tympanic membrane of the leopard gecko is externally visible on the side of the head, with no scale cover or flap over it.**  
[verified] Vedurmudi et al. use the leopard gecko as their type example of an internally coupled ear. Fig. 1 caption: the location of the eardrum / tympanic membrane on the side of its head is highlighted; a close-up of the head shows the eardrum and the embedded extracolumella can be 'discerned clearly'; a third panel is a close-up of the eardrum itself. This is photographic evidence for the species, not a verbal description. I extracted and read the PDF text directly. Caveat: the caption names only 'Leopard gecko', not the binomial, though that vernacular is standard for E. macularius.

- source: Vedurmudi AP, Goulet J, Christensen-Dalsgaard J, Young BA, Williams R, van Hemmen JL (2016) Internally coupled ears: mathematical structures and mechanisms underlying ICE. Biological Cybernetics 110: 359–382, Fig. 1 caption (p. 360). PDF: https://www.atsu.edu/research-portal/uploads/78040790-Internally%20coupled%20ears-mathematical%20structures.pdf
- for the model: Model the ear as a real aperture in the head silhouette with a visible membrane at its floor — not a painted-on spot and not a scale-covered depression.

## eye

**Eublepharids have genuinely movable eyelids; the family name is descriptive, not just etymological.**  
[verified] Boulenger's family diagnosis for Eublepharidae states the eyes are moderately large and are protected by thick, movable, lids that meet ("connivent"). Smith's genus diagnosis for Eublepharis independently states both eyelids are well developed and movable. Two independent primary taxonomic sources, ~50 years apart, agree. Smith elsewhere lists "connivent eyelids" as one of the three characters separating eublepharids from typical gekkonids (with opisthocoelous vertebrae and united parietals).

- source: Boulenger, G.A. 1885. Catalogue of the Lizards in the British Museum (Natural History), 2nd ed., Vol. 1, p. 229 (Fam. Eublepharidae). Archive.org id catalogueoflizar01brituoft. AND Smith, M.A. 1935. The Fauna of British India... Reptilia and Amphibia Vol. II, Sauria, pp. 125-126 (genus Eublepharis). Archive.org id in.ernet.dli.2015.207328.
- for the model: Model two real lid flaps as separate deformable geometry over the globe, not a painted-on ring. The lids are described as THICK, so they need volume — a fleshy fold with its own scale texture, not a thin membrane.

**Leopard geckos have NO spectacle / brille. The cornea is bare and directly exposed when the lids are open.**  
[verified] Smith states that in Eublepharis the eyeball "has lost the fixed membranous covering" that other geckos have. Hellebuyck & Solanes Vilanova state that a true transparent rigid spectacle is present in all Gekkota except the eublepharid clade, which has fully moveable eyelids. Reavill & Schmidt say it species-specifically: the leopard gecko "belongs to a family of geckos that have eyelids and not a spectacle" — and warn that the caseous corneal plug clinicians see in this species is commonly misdiagnosed as a retained spectacle. Three independent sources, one of them naming E. macularius directly.

- source: Smith 1935, Fauna of British India, Sauria, pp. 23-24. AND Hellebuyck, T. & Solanes Vilanova, F. 2023. Animals 13(6):1108, doi:10.3390/ani13061108. AND Reavill, D. & Schmidt, R.E. 2012. Pathology of the Reptile Eye and Ocular Adnexa. Proc. ARAV 2012, p. 89.
- for the model: Do NOT put a glossy transparent scale disc over the eye. The cornea is a wet, directly exposed, strongly specular dome — shade it as bare cornea with a sharp specular highlight, sitting inside a soft skin aperture, not behind a flat scale.

**The pupil is vertical.**  
[verified] Smith's genus diagnosis for Eublepharis lists flatly: "Pupil vertical." Boulenger's family diagnosis calls it an "elliptico-vertical pupil" — i.e. a vertically-elongate ellipse rather than a mathematical hairline. Two independent primary sources.

- source: Smith 1935, Fauna of British India, Sauria, p. 126. AND Boulenger 1885, Cat. Liz. Brit. Mus. Vol. 1, p. 229.
- for the model: Pupil axis is vertical in head space (perpendicular to the long axis of the eye aperture), so it stays vertical regardless of how the eye aperture is tilted on the skull.

## feet

**Each of the four feet bears five digits; there is no digit reduction.**  
[verified] Zhuang et al. (2019) figure 1B is a schematic of a gecko left pes in dorsal view explicitly 'based on a radiograph of the left pes of Eublepharis macularius', labelling five metatarsals MTI-MTV. Corroborated at genus level by congener descriptions that report subdigital lamellae as five per-digit counts for each autopodium (E. jhuma: right manus 10-15-17-18-15; right pes 11-16-21-23-22) and relative lengths for digits I-V.

- source: Zhuang, M.V., Russell, A.P. & Higham, T.E. (2019) Evolution of pedal digit orientation and morphology in relation to acquisition and secondary loss of the adhesive system in geckos. Journal of Morphology 280. PDF: https://biomechanics.ucr.edu/Zhuang_et_al-2019-Journal_of_Morphology.pdf — figure caption, p.4 of PDF text
- for the model: Model 5 toes per foot, 20 total. Digit I (innermost) is shortest on both hand and foot and sits noticeably offset from the others.

**Leopard geckos have NO adhesive toe pads: no expanded scansors, no adhesive pads, and no documented setae.**  
[verified] Peattie (2008) abstract, read verbatim via Europe PMC: 'Subdigital adhesive setae have previously been documented in all legged gecko families except the Eublepharidae.' The single exception she reports is Aeluroscalabotes felinus, 'the only arboreal eublepharids', with setae up to 9 micrometres — a different genus, not Eublepharis. Independently, Smith (1935, p.~119) states that Teratoscincus, Stenodactylus, Alsophylax, Gymnodactylus, Cnemaspis, Agamura, Pristurus 'and Eublepharis have the primitive, non-dilated form of digit. All the other genera mentioned in this work have adhesive expansions by means of which they are able to ascend vertical walls.' Smith's family key entry for the genus reads simply 'Digits not dilated, clawed ... Eublepharis, p. 125.'

- source: Peattie, A.M. (2008) Anatomical Record 291:869-875, DOI 10.1002/ar.20706 (abstract via Europe PMC REST API); Smith, M.A. (1935) The Fauna of British India, Reptilia and Amphibia Vol. II — Sauria, pp. 119, 125-128, https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.207328
- for the model: CRITICAL: do not model the fanned, flattened, pad-like toe undersides seen in Gekko or Hemidactylus references. The underside of each toe is a narrow, rounded strip, not a broadened paddle. No setal fringe, no leaf-like terminal pads.

**They DO have subdigital lamellae — the widespread claim that leopard geckos 'have no lamellae' is imprecise. What they lack is ADHESIVE pads/scansors/setae, not lamellae.**  
[verified] Boulenger (1890) genus diagnosis: 'Digits short and cylindrical, with a row of lamellae below, clawed...'. Smith (1935) genus diagnosis: 'Digits short, cylindrical, with transverse lamellae beneath, clawed...'. So a single ventral row of transverse plate-like scales runs the length of each toe; they simply are not adhesive scansors. This distinction is the single most commonly garbled fact about this animal.

- source: Boulenger, G.A. (1890) The Fauna of British India, Reptilia and Batrachia, Genus Eublepharis, p. 107, https://archive.org/details/TheFaunaOfBritishIndiaReptiliaAndBatrachia ; Smith, M.A. (1935) op. cit., p. 125
- for the model: Model a single row of transverse ventral scale-plates down each toe — visible as stacked bands on the sole of the toe. Roughly 15-23 per long toe based on congener counts. This is surface detail, not silhouette.

## head_scales

**The nostril is a lateral opening on the side of the snout very close to, but NOT touching, the rostral scale.**  
[verified] Boulenger (1885), in the family account for Eublepharidae, states the nostril is rather large, directed slightly upwards though lateral, and separated from the rostral and the labial plates. Smith (1935) states for E. hardwickii — and the E. macularius account on the following pages is written purely as a list of differences from hardwickii, none of them cranial — 'rostral broader than high, not in contact with the nostril'. Corroborated a third time by the modern Eublepharis descriptions, which place the naris inside a discrete nasal scale rather than on a suture. Three independent sources, two of them museum/fauna-level taxonomic works, agree. NOTE: the strongest statement (Boulenger) is a family-level diagnosis, and Smith's is inherited by E. macularius through the difference-only convention rather than being written out for it.

- source: Boulenger, G.A. 1885. Catalogue of the Lizards in the British Museum (Natural History), 2nd ed., Vol. I, family Eublepharidae, pp. 229–230 (read via archive.org OCR, item catalogueoflizar01brituoft); Smith, M.A. 1935. Fauna of British India, Reptilia and Amphibia Vol. II — Sauria, pp. 126–129 (archive.org item in.ernet.dli.2015.62460)
- for the model: Place the nostril aperture on the LATERAL face of the snout, roughly one scale-width behind the rostral plate, with the opening canted slightly dorsally rather than facing straight sideways. Critically: do NOT let the nostril hole touch or notch the rostral, and do not let it sit on the lip line. It is a hole within a single swollen scale, set above the first supralabial.

## tubercles

**The dorsal integument is built from exactly two visible element types: a pavement of small flattened scales, and larger raised tubercles set into that pavement.**  
[verified] Russell et al. (2014) SEM-surveyed skin from head, trunk, hindlimb and tail of adult leopard geckos (SVL 89-120 mm, n=5 for body regions, 7 original + 2 regenerated tails) and describe the dorsum as clad in "two general types of integumentary derivative": scales (granular, semi-imbricating or imbricating) and tubercles. Corroborated independently by Smith (1935, p.126-127), who describes the genus as "back with small juxtaposed scales and larger tubercles", and by Boulenger (1885, p.230-232), whose key splits Eublepharis into "back with enlarged tubercles" (incl. macularius) vs "back uniformly granular".

- source: Russell AP, Lai EK, Powell GL, Higham TE (2014) Journal of Morphology 275:961-979, doi:10.1002/jmor.20269 (PDF: https://biomechanics.ucr.edu/Russell_etal_2014.pdf), Results "Scale Descriptions"; Smith MA (1935) Fauna of British India, Reptilia & Amphibia Vol. II Sauria, pp.126-128; Boulenger GA (1885) Catalogue of the Lizards in the British Museum (Nat. Hist.) Vol. I, pp.230-232
- for the model: Two-layer surface: a continuous fine base tiling plus a sparse scattering of discrete raised bumps. Do not model the bumps as a modulation of the base tiling - they are separate, larger elements that displace it.

**The specific number of dorsal tubercle rows at midbody for E. macularius sensu stricto is not in any source I could open; the nearest figure is 18 rows, for the Rajasthan/Delhi population.**  
[likely] Mirza (2024) describes the Aravalli/Delhi population (as E. madarensis, resurrected; The Reptile Database still treats madarensis as a synonym of E. macularius and disputes the revalidation) with "large unkeeled tubercles in 18 longitudinal rows" and, in the specimen description, "the large scales are arranged in 18 longitudinal rows at midbody". Comparative counts in the same papers: E. satpuraensis 20 rows; E. hardwickii and E. pictus 23-26 rows of flat tubercle-like scales. The likely source of a macularius count, Mirza et al. (2014) Phyllomedusa 13(2):75-90, was unreachable (revistas.usp.br refused connection from this network), so I did not open it and do not cite it.

- source: Mirza ZA (2024) Taprobanica 13(1):9-15, Diagnosis and Description (PDF: https://www.taprobanica.org/~file/318_eublepharis_madarensis_taprobanica_13_1_2024_mirza_9_15-99e7c-3349_1243.pdf); Reptile Database account for E. macularius, https://reptile-database.reptarium.cz/species?genus=Eublepharis&species=macularius (Comment section, on the status of madarensis)
- for the model: Budget roughly 18-20 tubercle rows spanning the dorsum from flank to flank at midbody. Treat this as the right order of magnitude, not a verified count for macularius.

## Refuted or corrected

- **Tubercles never touch each other: each is isolated from its neighbours by intervening small scales.** -- REFUTED AS WORDED, BY ITS OWN CITED SOURCE. I opened both sources (source_opened = true for both).

1. THE HEDGE THE CLAIM DROPS. Russell, Lai, Powell & Higham (2014) J. Morphol. 275:961-979, Results ("Scale Descriptions", p. 968) reads exactly: "Tubercles juxtapose neighboring scales and are usually isolated from other tubercles by intervening scales." The qualifier "usually" is the authors' own. It entails exceptions. The claim converts this into "never" and "each", which is a strictly stronger assertion than the source makes. I searched the full text for "never", "always", "exclusive", "without exception" — the authors never make an absolute statement about tubercle isolation. The submitted DETAIL actually quotes "usually" correctly, then the CLAIM headline silently strengthens it; the failure is in the claim, not the transcription. PDF opened at https://biomechanics.ucr.edu/Russell_etal_2014.pdf and text-extracted locally to C:\Users\ziyad\AppData\Local\Temp\claude\C--Users-ziyad\920e259d-472d-4210-945a-591bc20b749a\scratchpad\russell2014_flow.txt

2. SCOPE ERROR. That sentence sits in the paragraph describing the DORSAL BODY (referencing Fig. 2c), not a whole-animal rule. Russell et al. state separately that "The ventral regions of the body are devoid of tubercles."

3. REGIONS WHERE "EACH IS ISOLATED" HAS NO PURCHASE. Russell et al.: ventrally on the tail "the tubercles also lose their identity along this transitional gradient"; in segment 5 "distinction between tubercles, imbricating scales and ventral plates is lost"; and "The regenerated tail lacks tubercles and bears scales that are more homogeneous than those of the original." A regenerated tail is common in both wild and captive animals, so a universal quantifier over "each tubercle" fails outright there.

4. A WORDING SLIP THAT INVERTS THE ANATOMY. Russell et al. use "juxtapose" to mean abut/lie edge-to-edge (contrast their imbricating scales, which overlap). So a tubercle IS in direct contact with the small scales around it ("Tubercles juxtapose neighboring scales"); what it is usually separated from is OTHER TUBERCLES. The claim's "isolated from its neighbours by intervening small scales" garbles this — the intervening scales are the neighbours, not a gap between the tubercle and its neighbours.

5. WHAT DOES HOLD, AND IT CORROBORATES TWICE. The weaker, hedged observation is genuinely supported by two independently opened sources. McLean KE & Vickaryous MK (2011) BMC Dev Biol 11:50 (opened at the PMC URL given): "Along the dorsal and lateral surfaces this pavement of flattened scales is interrupted at regular intervals by large cone-like tubercles" and "Close inspection reveals that adjacent, slightly enlarged scales surround each tubercle." The DETAIL's characterisation of McLean & Vickaryous is accurate. Russell et al. add that on the tail tubercles are "spaced dorsoventrally in columns, at relatively regular intervals" and (Fig. 4 caption) "arranged in columns ... within whorls, and rows ... along the tail" — regular spacing, consistent with usual separation.

6. SPECIES CONFUSION — CHECKED, NONE FOUND. Both papers genuinely study E. macularius; I found no sign the observation was imported from another gecko. The E. pictus material (Mirza & Gnaneswar 2022, rosette of 10-12 small scales) is a congener transfer and the submitter already flags it as such, which is correct practice. I did NOT open Mirza & Gnaneswar 2022, so the 10-12 rosette count is UNVERIFIED here and must not be cited as E. macularius.

7. CAPTIVE-BRED VS WILD-TYPE — A REAL UNRESOLVED CAVEAT. McLean & Vickaryous state explicitly: "All E. macularius were captive bred and obtained from one of two commercial suppliers (Pat Wise Lizards, Calgary, Alberta, Canada; Global Exotic Pets, Kitchener, Ontario, Canada)." They give no wild-type or morph designation. Russell et al. give NO provenance at all — only "Seven original tails and two fully regenerated ... tails were removed from specimens (snout-vent length 89-120 mm) of leopard geckos" under Calgary protocol BI07R-32; no wild-caught/captive-bred statement, no locality, no morph. So neither opened source documents wild-caught wild-type animals, and the tail half of the claim rests wholly on captive-bred commercial pet-trade stock. COULD_NOT_VERIFY: whether wild E. macularius show the same tubercle spacing, and whether captive selective breeding alters tubercle arrangement — I have no source open on either, and I am not filling that gap. Treat any "wild-type" framing of this trait as UNVERIFIED.
  - correction: Replace the absolute with the sourced hedge, and fix the neighbour/tubercle inversion:

"On the dorsal body, tubercles abut the surrounding small scales directly and are USUALLY — not invariably — separated from OTHER TUBERCLES by those intervening scales (Russell et al. 2014, J. Morphol. 275:961-979, Results: 'Tubercles juxtapose neighboring scales and are usually isolated from other tubercles by intervening scales'). On the original tail the same pattern holds: a pavement of small flattened scales is interrupted at regular intervals by large cone-like tubercles, with adjacent slightly enlarged scales surrounding each tubercle (McLean & Vickaryous 2011, BMC Dev Biol 11:50), the tubercles being spaced in columns within whorls and in rows along the tail (Russell et al. 2014). This description does not extend to the whole animal: the ventral body is devoid of tubercles, tubercles lose their identity ventrolaterally on the tail and are indistinguishable from other scales near the tail tip, and regenerated tails lack tubercles entirely (Russell et al. 2014)."

Confidence: the hedged version is MODERATE-to-GOOD (corroborated by two opened, peer-reviewed sources). The original absolute ("never", "each") is UNVERIFIED and contradicted by the qualifier in its own primary source. Provenance caveat to carry forward: both sources used captive animals (McLean & Vickaryous explicitly captive-bred commercial stock; Russell et al. unstated), so wild-type applicability is UNVERIFIED.

RELATED-SPECIES TRANSFER (the core defect)
Every published longitudinal-row count I could open is for a congener, never for E. macularius: E. hardwickii 24 rows and E. pictus 23-26 (Mirza & Gnaneswar 2022); E. satpuraensis "~20 rows" (Mirza, Sanap, Raju, Gawai & Ghadekar 2014, Phyllomedusa 13(2):75-90, opened, ...\scratchpad\satpura.txt); E. madarensis 18 (Mirza 2024). These are congeners in the same genus but different species, and the transfer is especially unsafe here because row-describability tracks exactly the character that separates them: Mirza & Gnaneswar 2022 diagnose hardwickii + pictus as having flat tubercle-like scales with "interspaces much less than the size of the scales", versus "interspaces much more than the size of the tubercles in E. angramainyu ..., E. fuscus ..., E. macularius (Blyth), E. satpuraensis ... and E. turcmenicus". Densely packed rosette-set tubercles are countable in rows; the widely spaced macularius condition is what the row-count was not applied to.

THE SECOND HALF OF THE CLAIM ALSO FAILS
"No author describes them as a lattice" is an unverifiable universal negative, and the evidence inside the genus runs the other way: "nearly equidistant from each other and cover the dorsum" (Mirza 2024, madarensis) is a statement of even spacing, and "each large scale is enveloped in a rosette of 10-12 small scales" (Mirza 2022, pictus) — matched by "10-12 smaller scales surround each dorsal tubercle" in an E. angramainyu description (opened, ...\scratchpad\arc_angramainyu.txt) — is a regular repeating unit, i.e. as lattice-like as this literature gets. The absence of the word "lattice" is a fact about vocabulary, not about the skin.
The gloss of "fairly arranged" as "approximate, not strict" is also the claimant's, not the author's: Mirza uses the same stock phrase for geckos with genuinely regular rows, e.g. "enlarged, keeled, tubercles, fairly regularly arranged in 15-16 longitudinal rows on dorsum ... individual tubercle row separated from the adjacent by three transverse scale rows" in a Hemidactylus description (opened, ...\scratchpad\arc.txt), explicitly contrasted there with congeners whose tubercles are "irregularly arranged". In his usage "fairly (regularly) arranged" means regular enough to score, not loose.

Corroboration status: the DTR Methods sentence is doubly corroborated (both Mirza papers, opened). The absence of trunk-row description for E. macularius is corroborated four times (Boulenger 1885, Boulenger 1890, Smith 1935, Russell et al. 2014). The row counts for congeners each rest on a single source per species.
  - correction: What the opened sources actually support, restricted to E. macularius: the trunk bears small, irregular, juxtaposed scales intermixed with enlarged rounded-to-subconical, smooth or feebly keeled tubercles whose interspaces are as wide as or wider than the tubercles themselves (Boulenger 1885:232; Boulenger 1890:108; Smith 1935:127-128), and under SEM those trunk tubercles "juxtapose neighboring scales and are usually isolated from other tubercles by intervening scales" (Russell et al. 2014:968). None of these authors describes the trunk tubercles as forming rows of any orientation; all row and column language in this literature — Boulenger 1890, Smith 1935 and Russell et al. 2014 alike — is reserved for the tail, where tubercles sit in dorsoventral columns within each caudal whorl "at relatively regular intervals" and in longitudinal rows along the tail's length.

Counting longitudinal tubercle rows (DTR, "counted from across the dorsum of the trunk") is indeed a standard meristic in modern Eublepharis taxonomy (Mirza & Gnaneswar 2022; Mirza 2024), but every published count I could find is for another species: E. hardwickii 24, E. pictus 23-26, E. satpuraensis ~20 (Mirza et al. 2014), E. madarensis 18 (Mirza 2024 — a taxon that paper removes from E. macularius). The only publication I found that defines DTR for animals identified as E. macularius (Rawat et al. 2019, Nepal) leaves the value unscored. Notably, Mirza & Gnaneswar's own diagnosis places E. macularius in the widely-spaced-tubercle group (interspaces much greater than tubercle size), opposite the closely packed, rosette-set tubercles of the species for which row counts are given.

So: whether E. macularius trunk tubercles form countable longitudinal series — loosely or otherwise — is UNVERIFIED. Do not assert rows on the trunk of this species; do not carry the "nearly equidistant" wording over from E. madarensis or "fairly arranged in 26 longitudinal rows" from E. pictus, and drop the unverifiable "no author describes them as a lattice", which in any case sits awkwardly with the even-spacing and rosette wording that does appear for congeners.

WHAT REFUTES THE INFERENCE:
(a) Blyth 1854, J. Asiat. Soc. The claim's key move — "three historical sources on wild material agree on brown" — fails: the first and most directly wild-derived source says black.
(b) The three brown sources are not independent. Boulenger's specimen list on p. 232 includes entries e-f from Hyderabad, Sind, presented by R. T. (c) All three brown sources describe spirit-preserved museum specimens, where melanin-based black commonly reads brown and ground colour fades. (d) The brown/black split does not track adults or captivity. Ullate-Agote et al. call the HATCHLING bands black, where Günther/Boulenger/Smith call the juvenile bands brown. The terminological difference runs through the whole ontogeny, so it cannot be attributed to adult captive stock.
(e) Neither side of the contrast measures anything. Ullate-Agote's "wild-type" is a lab line of unstated wild provenance in a colony maintained alongside the Mack Super Snow morph, so their "black" is not a wild-animal observation either.

(1) There is no modern redescription of E. macularius giving its own ear-opening shape, orientation and EL/OD ratio. Every Eublepharis description with those numbers (Mirza et al. 2014; Mirza & Gnaneswar 2022; Mirza 2024; Mohapatra et al. 2026) is for a congener. For E. macularius the only species-specific figure I found is Grismer's 'height of auditory meatus 1.5 times internostril distance', and I read that in the Reptile Database compilation, not in Grismer (1988), which is a Stanford University Press book chapter I could not obtain. The classical descriptions (Boulenger 1885, 1890; Smith 1935) write the head and ear characters in the E. hardwickii account and then list E. macularius as differing only in tubercle size, preanal pore count and body/digit proportions — so those ear statements reach macularius by the authors' own 'differs from the preceding by...' construction, not as direct observations of macularius. I have flagged that everywhere it applies.

(2) No absolute dimensions. I found no published ear-opening length or height in millimetres for E. macularius. Mirza (2024) does tabulate EL and OD in mm, but the columns in the extracted PDF table are misaligned against their row labels and I will not quote numbers I cannot align with confidence. Werner & Wever (1972), 'The function of the middle ear in lizards: Gekko gecko and Eublepharis macularius', does report tympanic membrane areas for this species, but Wiley returned HTTP 403 and I could not read it; I am therefore not quoting any membrane area.

(3) Meatus depth. I could not find a measured depth for the E. macularius external auditory meatus. 'Ear opening deep' is Mirza et al.'s wording for E. satpuraensis; 'a shallow external ear canal' is Christensen-Dalsgaard & Manley's for Gekko gecko. Those two are not obviously consistent, and neither is about macularius. Treat the recession as qualitative: present, a few millimetres, not quantified.

(4) See-through head. The red-laser-through-the-head demonstration is published for Gekko gecko only. Gekko is in Gekkonidae, a different family from Eublepharidae; the split between them is deep within Gekkota, so this is a distant transfer, not a near relative. I found no peer-reviewed source showing or stating it for E. macularius. Pet-care and hobbyist pages assert it confidently; those are weak sources and I did not count them.

(5) Exact anatomical direction of the extracolumella bar. The leopard gecko photograph caption places the pale extracolumella protrusion at the 'top-left' of the tympanum, but that is an image coordinate and I cannot tell which side of the head was photographed or how it was oriented, so I cannot say whether that is anterodorsal or posterodorsal on the animal.

(6) Whether E. macularius has the three conical tubercles on the anterior wall of the ear opening that Mirza (2024) describes for E. madarensis. Unrecorded either way.

(7) I could not open: Grismer (1988); Wever (1978) The Reptile Ear; Werner & Wever (1972); Werner et al. (2005, 2008); Anderson (1999) The Lizards of Iran. Several of these are the actual primary sources behind statements I have cited at one remove, and each is flagged as such in the relevant finding.

One correction to the premise in the brief: geckos do not have unusually open or superficial ear canals. The opposite is the case. Evans's lepidosaur ear chapter treats the flush, near-surface tympanum as the common lizard condition and singles out geckos as a group in which the membrane is recessed more deeply below the surface and protected by a meatal closure muscle. What is true is that geckos have a large, conspicuous, unscaled external ear opening — visible, but set at the bottom of a short funnel.
- **feet**: Five things I could not document and am explicitly NOT filling in:

1. WEBBING. No source I opened — Boulenger (1890), Smith (1935), the Reptile Database diagnosis, or any of the three modern Eublepharis species descriptions — mentions interdigital webbing for Eublepharis, in either direction. Gecko genus diagnoses do routinely mention webbing when it is present (e.g. Smith's treatment of digit form across genera is detailed), so silence is weak evidence of absence, and Zhuang et al. (2019) measure free interdigital angles in the E. macularius pes, which implies separate digits. But no source states "webbing absent". Treat as: almost certainly no significant webbing, formally undocumented. Model toes as free, with at most a low basal skin fold.

2. TOE PATTERNING / BANDING. I found no description of digit or foot colouration for E. macularius. Boulenger (1890) and Smith (1935) describe head, body and tail pattern in detail and say nothing about the limbs or feet. The closest datum is the congener E. jhuma: "Limbs paler than body color with whitish spots on the legs" (Mohapatra et al. 2026) — and that refers to the legs, not the toes. Whether E. macularius toes are banded, speckled or plain is unverified. Do not invent body-style banding on the toes.

3. E. MACULARIUS-SPECIFIC DIGIT MEASUREMENTS. Powell, G.L., Osgood, G.J. & Russell, A.P. (2018) "Ontogenetic allometry of the digital rays of the leopard gecko (Gekkota: Eublepharidae; Eublepharis macularius)", Acta Zoologica 99:331-356, DOI 10.1111/azo.12215 is exactly the paper for toe proportions — metapodial and digit scaling in manus and pes across ontogeny. It is paywalled at Wiley; the Wiley page is behind a bot-verification wall which I did not attempt to bypass, ResearchGate returned 403, and no open repository copy exists. I confirmed its existence and full citation via the Crossref API only. All my digit-proportion figures are therefore transferred from congeners. If you want E. macularius numbers, this is the paper to obtain.

4. SUBDIGITAL LAMELLA COUNT FOR E. MACULARIUS. I have counts for congeners (E. jhuma 22-25 on the fourth toe; E. hardwickii 17; E. pictus 19; E. satpuraensis 18-22; E. turcmenicus 20-23; E. madarensis 17-19) but the diagnoses I opened never state the E. macularius range, because tuberculate-vs-smooth texture rather than count is the character used to separate it. A figure in the 15-25 range is a reasonable interpolation but I am not asserting it.

5. WHICH SPECIES KOPPETSCH ET AL. (2020) EXAMINED. The abstract says "three species of Eublepharidae" without naming them; full text paywalled. Search-engine summaries asserted the genera were Aeluroscalabotes, Goniurosaurus and Eublepharis, but I did not open a source confirming that, so I have not treated it as established.

PROCESS NOTE ON SOURCE QUALITY: the two strongest sources here are Boulenger (1890) and Smith (1935), both read as full scanned text from the Internet Archive. They are old but are formal taxonomic diagnoses and they agree with each other independently and with modern osteological work (Russell & Gamble 2019). No pet-care or breeder sites were used for any claim. The single most important correction to the common framing: leopard geckos DO have subdigital lamellae — a row of transverse tuberculate plates — they lack adhesive pads, scansors and setae. Sources that say "no lamellae" are wrong.
- **head_scales**: Gaps, stated plainly rather than filled in.

1. NO MODERN FULL REDESCRIPTION OF E. MACULARIUS EXISTS THAT I COULD OPEN. This is the central problem. Every detailed modern Eublepharis head description I found (Mirza & Gnaneswar 2022; Mirza 2024) is of a congener — E. hardwickii, E. pictus or E. madarensis. The only full head scalation text that applies to E. macularius is Smith (1935) and Boulenger (1885/1890), and in both works the E. macularius account is written purely as a short list of differences from E. hardwickii (dorsal tubercle size, preanal pore count, proportions, colour) — so every head character is inherited by taxonomic convention rather than written out for the species. I have flagged each inherited statement.

2. EXTENT OF THE ROSTRAL'S MEDIAN GROOVE IS CONTRADICTORY. Boulenger 1885/1890 say the cleft is 'above' (upper part only); Mirza's congener descriptions say the rostral is 'divided by a median suture for its entire length'. I could not determine which applies to E. macularius. If the groove depth matters to your model, treat it as a choice.

3. NO MALE-vs-FEMALE HEAD WIDTH NUMBERS FOR E. MACULARIUS. I could not obtain any paper reporting mean head width by sex in millimetres for this species. Kratochvíl & Frynta (2002) is the right source and I read its abstract, but the species table is paywalled, so I never saw the E. macularius row confirming male-biased HSD for this species specifically. Several search-result snippets asserted 'males have wider heads than females' attributing it to Gutzke & Crews 1988, but the aggregator page carrying that claim returned HTTP 403 and I never opened it, and the Gutzke & Crews 1988 Nature abstract I did read is about temperature-dependent sex determination and does not mention head width. I have therefore NOT cited it. The male head widening is reported as 'likely', resting on the family-level combat/HSD argument plus verified male-male aggression in E. macularius.

4. THE JAW-ANGLE LOCATION OF THE WIDENING IS INFERRED, NOT DOCUMENTED. No source I opened says 'the head widens at the jaw angle in mature males'. The inference chain is: morphometric protocols define head width as the largest width of the head; enlarged male heads function as biting weapons; bite force comes from the jaw adductor chamber, which lies at the jaw angle. Reasonable, but it is my reasoning, not a citation.

5. THE VISIBLE MOUTH LINE IS NOT DOCUMENTED ANYWHERE I LOOKED. Marked unverified above. Only indirect support (labials counted separately per jaw, 'to angle of jaw').

6. NO SOURCE STATES WHETHER E. MACULARIUS LABIALS ARE PALER AS A RULE. The closest is a single statement about E. madarensis labials lacking the head's reticulation, plus the verified negative that E. macularius lacks the pale supralabial stripe that E. satpuraensis and E. hardwickii have. Single-source, transferred, flagged.

SPECIFIC GAPS: (a) No published quantification of spot size, count or density for WILD adult E. macularius exists among the sources I read. The only quantification I found (Jančúchová-Lásková et al. 2015) covers HEAD spots only and reports square-root- and log-transformed values whose underlying table I could not extract. My spot-size figures are my own estimate from one magnified photograph and are flagged unverified. (b) No source I opened states in words that the adult tail is paler than the body; I inferred it from the chromatophore histology plus photographs. (c) No source gives the relative TIMING of body-band versus tail-band breakup; the observation that the tail retains bands longer rests on three photographs of subadults. (d) I found no statement about whether wild adults ever retain complete tail rings into full adulthood. (e) I could not verify whether ground colour shifts with the shed cycle, time of day or temperature — three Kirthar animals looked strikingly pale pinkish-lilac compared with the Sindh lowland animals and I do not know whether that is population, shed state, or flash photography at night. (f) I did not verify geographic variation in adult ground colour across the range in any systematic way.

SOURCE-STRENGTH CAVEATS: The iNaturalist observations are wild animals in the native range, and I opened and viewed every photograph I cite — but they are community-identified, not expert-vouchered museum specimens, so they are weaker than the taxonomic literature. I weighted the Pakistani records (including one from Chiddru in the Salt Range, effectively the type locality) above the Indian ones. Günther 1864 describes his material under the name Eublepharis fasciolatus; I treat it as E. macularius only because both Boulenger 1885 and Smith 1935 — which I opened — place fasciolatus in the synonymy of macularius, and its type locality (Hyderabad, Sindh) is well within the range. No fact in this report has been transferred from a different Eublepharis species; where E. hardwickii or E. angramainyu appear above it is only as an explicit contrast, never as a substitute.
